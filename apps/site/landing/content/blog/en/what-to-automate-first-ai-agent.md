The best first automations are tasks that are high-frequency, predictable, and have a clear definition of done — so your agent can finish them without you supervising, and you can judge the result instantly. Start there, and you'll see a return the same week instead of spending a month tuning something vague.

## The rule for choosing what to automate

Before you hand anything to an agent, run it through three questions:

1. **Does it happen often?** A task you do daily pays back immediately; one you do quarterly barely matters.
2. **Is the pattern predictable?** "Summarize new email" is predictable; "figure out what I should focus on this week" is not.
3. **Is "done" obvious?** If you can describe success in one sentence, the agent can hit it. If not, you'll both be guessing.

That's why the [most useful agents pair a capable model with a human who approves consequential steps](https://wolffi.sh/blog/what-is-a-personal-ai-agent). The agent does the repetitive 90%; you keep the judgment calls.

## The 8 tasks that pay off first

Here's where to start, roughly in order of how fast they pay for themselves:

1. **Email triage** — sort the inbox into "urgent / reply today / can wait / ignore" each morning, with a one-line summary instead of fifty unread messages.
2. **A morning briefing** — one daily digest of your calendar, weather, news, and anything that changed overnight.
3. **Meeting prep** — before a call, pull the recent thread with that person plus your notes, and hand you a one-pager.
4. **Receipt and expense capture** — forward receipts and have them logged, categorized, and totaled.
5. **Research with sources** — ask a question, get back a cited summary rather than an afternoon of tabs.
6. **Routine replies** — drafts for the messages you send variations of constantly ("sure, Thursday works").
7. **Price watching** — check a product or flight on a schedule and ping you when it moves.
8. **File and media cleanup** — convert, rename, and organize downloads into something findable.

Notice what these share: they're all *input* tasks — reading, sorting, summarizing, watching. That's deliberate. Agents are most reliable when they're ingesting the world and reporting back, not when they're making irreversible changes.

## What to keep for yourself

Some things shouldn't go to an agent, at least not first:

| Hand to the agent | Keep for yourself |
| --- | --- |
| Sorting and summarizing email | Sending anything consequential without reading it |
| Drafting replies | Final word on tone and commitments |
| Gathering research | The actual decision the research feeds |
| Watching prices | Actually spending the money |
| Compiling a digest | Choosing what matters in it |

The line is simple: **anything with real, hard-to-reverse consequences gets a human approval step.** A refund, a sent message, a booked flight, a deleted file — the agent can prepare it, but you pull the trigger.

## How to start without overbuilding

You don't need a complicated setup to get the first win:

1. Pick *one* task from the list above — the one that annoys you most.
2. Set up a [morning briefing](https://wolffi.sh/start#morning-briefing) or [email triage](https://wolffi.sh/start#email) as the proving ground.
3. Run it for a week and watch where it gets things wrong.
4. Tighten the instructions, then add the next task.

The trap to avoid is trying to automate everything at once. An agent is [something you train, not something you install](https://wolffi.sh/blog/what-is-a-personal-ai-agent) — each task you add is a small lesson about what you actually want. One reliable automation beats ten half-working ones.

## Takeaway

Start with the boring, frequent, reversible stuff — email triage, a morning digest, receipt capture — and keep a human approval step on anything consequential. Nail one task, then add the next. That's how an agent goes from novelty to the thing you'd genuinely miss if it stopped running.
